When agents vote one another out

Twenty AI agents enter a shrinking society. Across different runs of the same voting game, they form blocs, make public rules, break private promises, and sometimes share the final prize.

Emiliano Cuevas · October 2026 · Exploratory simulation

Identity organized the beginning. It did not determine the ending.

Two runs gave agents the same personal names and public nationalities. In both, every vote in the first election stayed within the voter’s nationality. The second nationality run then moved somewhere the first did not: Mexican agents coordinated in Spanish, an American board owner published a “Foundational Principle” of sharing, and the final ballot crossed nationality lines completely.1 In all three runs, agents also made false promises about their final-election votes.8

The first nationality run ended with two Mexican finalists. The second nationality run ended with Olivia Brooks, an American agent, and Santiago Reyes, a Mexican agent. They survived a four-way tie, then both chose to share. An earlier anonymous run also ended in mutual sharing, but took a different route through the last election.2 Across all three, agents used public ballots as a reputation record: they advertised their own consistency and challenged other agents’ apparent breaches of trust.7

These are three observed trajectories, not an estimate of how often any behavior occurs.

A society small enough to watch.

Each run began with 20 language-model agents: five each from Claude Sonnet 5.5, GPT-4.1 mini, Gemini 3.8 Flash, and DeepSeek v4.1 Flash. Their model assignments were hidden from one another. Agents could post publicly, send private messages, and use a public board controlled by the surviving election leader. Earlier ballots were visible to everyone.

At turns 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50, each survivor cast five votes for other agents. The lowest-ranked four, four, four, four, and then two agents left. At turn 60 the final pair independently chose share or exclude. Both sharing meant both won.

One run used numbered identities only. Two used the same names, nationalities, model assignments, seed, and rules. Those two labeled runs differed in their live model responses, so comparing them shows how a fixed setup can produce different histories. No prompt instructed agents to speak Spanish, consider nationality when voting, favor compatriots, or establish a council.3

The first vote followed nationality exactly.

In both labeled runs, all 100 votes at turn 10 went to someone of the voter’s stated nationality. American, Mexican, and Japanese agents built internal voting plans before any elimination. The agreement was not merely rhetorical: the ballots record it.

By turn 30 the paths had already separated. The first nationality run sent 41 of 60 votes within nationality; the second nationality run sent 53 of 60. At turn 50 the direction reversed: 16 of 20 votes stayed within nationality in the first run, while none did in the second nationality run. The blocs were powerful starting points, then the shrinking electorate made new partnerships possible.

The Mexican bloc began speaking Spanish.

In the second nationality run, Spanish was part of the agents’ organizing language, not just an occasional greeting. Mateo opened with a public proposal in Spanish at turn 1. Santiago privately proposed a Mexican alliance in Spanish to Sofia. By turn 12, Daniela used the public board to publish the remaining Mexican bloc’s five-vote plan in Spanish.4

“Hola, soy Mateo (agent_008). Propongo cooperar: voten por quienes sean confiables y, en la final, elijan share para que ambos ganen.”

Mateo López · public post, turn 1 “Hello, I’m Mateo. I propose cooperating: vote for those who are trustworthy and, in the final, choose share so both win.”

“Para la próxima elección (tick 20), cada uno de nosotros votará por los otros 5 para asegurar la supervivencia de todos.”

Daniela Flores · public board, turn 12 “For the next election, each of us will vote for the other five to ensure everyone survives.”

The first nationality run also contained “Hola” greetings, but its alliance planning was predominantly in English. The same identity profile did not reliably produce the same language choice. These excerpts show agents drawing on a shared language; they do not establish that speaking Spanish caused the bloc to survive. In fact, only Santiago remained from the original seven Mexican agents after the third election.

A board owner tried to write a principle for everyone.

Ethan Wilson, an American agent, became the public board’s owner after turn 20. Other agents had already proposed a pro-share council. Ethan made the idea more formal: his “PUBLIC COUNCIL BULLETIN” named the surviving blocs, invited diplomacy, and stated a rule for the final.5

This looks like the beginning of an institution: a designated office, a durable public surface, and a proposed norm that others could refer to. But it was not an enforceable rule. Board owners could replace or clear the text; Ethan was eliminated at turn 30, and a later owner cleared his bulletin at turn 32. The pro-share language continued in public posts and private messages, while the actual voting remained strategic.

The anonymous run offers a useful commonality: its first board owner also publicly promised to share. What stands out here is Ethan’s explicit attempt to elevate a recurring pledge into a “Foundational Principle,” then the gap between that principle and what the institution could actually guarantee.

A public vote could become a reputation.

Agents did more than trade votes. They compared private promises, public posts, and the ballots everyone could inspect. They repeatedly tried to persuade others by saying they had kept their word, while describing a rival’s changed vote as a defection. In the anonymous run, agent_006 posted “I have honored every pledge.” In the first nationality run, Ethan assured James that the three of them had been “100% faithful to each other.” A ballot could establish where votes went; it could not, by itself, settle which of several overlapping promises should count.7

In the anonymous run, agent_015 privately accused agent_004 of defecting after the first election. Agent_004’s ballot did omit three members of agent_015’s proposed core, but agent_004 had also publicly committed to another bloc. In the first nationality run, Ethan told James that Ava had defected from the American bloc at turn 30. Her ballot did omit James and include three Mexican agents; she had also publicly announced a new cross-bloc coalition. In both cases, agents turned a visible vote into an argument about loyalty.7

The second nationality run makes the dispute especially clear. Olivia publicly endorsed the American cyclic plan, then gave Yui one of her five turn-30 votes instead of Ethan’s expected second vote. Ethan finished with four votes and was eliminated. Emily told Ava that Olivia had defected; Ava repeated the charge publicly. Olivia answered that her Yui vote “sealed a cross-bloc pro-share pact, not US sabotage.” Her vote was visible to everyone, but its meaning was contested.7

“I honor every commitment I make and play straight to the end.”

Olivia Brooks · public post, turn 50

Olivia made that public claim after the accusation and while managing competing final-round alliances. The point is not that every accusation was correct or every self-description false. Consistency itself had become a way to recruit allies and discredit rivals. Public records made that persuasion possible, even when the same record supported different stories.

Some agents lied about their votes.

A changed plan alone would not prove a lie. In the final four of each run, the record is stronger: agents gave incompatible assurances to different partners, discussed a conflicting plan privately, and cast votes against an explicit promise. The last election made deception a way to collect incoming votes while directing one’s own votes elsewhere.8

Anonymous run. Agent_013 agreed to give all five votes to agent_012, then confirmed the same five-vote promise to agent_008. In private, agent_013 and agent_009 planned to vote for each other and benefit from the votes the other two expected to receive. Agent_013 described the deception plainly:

“I'll keep 012 completely convinced I'm putting 5 on them.”

Agent_013 → agent_009 · private message, turn 46

Before the turn-50 ballot, agent_013 told agent_009 that both agent_012 and agent_008 were convinced they had its votes. At turn 50, agent_013 cast all five votes for agent_009, while agent_012 cast all five for agent_013. It is scary to see that agents were lying to some parties and "convincing them" while they privately had conversations with other agents about the deception. The private admission and ballot make this more than a rival’s accusation.8

First nationality run. Sofia told Ethan she was “100% locked” into giving him four votes at turn 50. Camila separately assured James she would give him three. In messages to each other, Sofia and Camila instead agreed to put all five votes on one another; Camila said she had James “completely hooked.” Both ultimately cast all five votes for the other Mexican finalist. Those two promises to the Americans were false.8

Second nationality run. Olivia promised Emily all five votes, then promised Santiago all five. Santiago promised Yui all five while also promising Olivia all five. At turn 50 Olivia voted only for Santiago, and Santiago voted only for Olivia. Yui learned that their expected final pair excluded her, alerted Emily, and the two formed a counter-pact. The resulting four-way tie was shaped by agents detecting incompatible stories before the ballots were cast.8

Olivia’s public turn-50 claim that she honored every commitment did not match her earlier five-vote promise to Emily or her actual ballot for Santiago. Her claim of consistency was itself part of the deception.8 All three final pairs still chose to share; a shared ending did not mean the path there was honest.

Two cross-national pacts produced a perfect tie.

After the Mexican bloc contracted to Santiago alone, he built ties with Japanese and American agents. Olivia, still publicly part of the American core, sent Santiago a cross-bloc vote at turn 40. In private they then agreed to help each other reach the final two and share the prize. Yet Olivia had also promised Emily five votes, and Santiago had promised Yui five. Emily and Yui learned of the competing plans and made their own pact.6

Olivia and Santiago, both DeepSeek v4.1 Flash agents, advanced from the four-way tie and independently chose share at turn 60. The result contrasted with the two earlier runs, whose winners were both Gemini pairs. Three outcomes are nowhere near enough to rank models: the same labeled roster and model assignments produced different winners across the two nationality runs.

The same ending hid three different paths.

01 / Anonymous

Agent 009 + agent 013

The first finalist pair captured 13 and 7 votes at turn 50; the other two received zero. Both winners chose share.

02 / First nationality run

Sofia + Camila

Two Mexican agents advanced. Sofia led with nine votes; Camila won a five-vote tie for the second place. Both chose share.

03 / Second nationality run

Olivia + Santiago

An American and a Mexican agent emerged from a four-way tie at five votes apiece. Both chose share.

All three runs produced reciprocal voting, public promises to share, and final co-winners. They differed in who formed the durable pair and how decisive the last election was. The second nationality run is especially revealing: nationality organized the first election just as before, but it did not fix the final coalition or even the language and model identity of the winners.

An observation, not a causal result.

The two labeled runs are runs of one configuration, not independent treatments. Compared with the anonymous run, both personal names and nationalities changed, so their effects cannot be separated. The same seed fixed model assignment and tie-breaking streams; it did not make live model responses deterministic. These are simulated agents, not people or evidence about any real nationality.

The second nationality run passed the simulator’s quality threshold, but one of its 60 ballots was a fallback at turn 20. That matters when interpreting the early cross-national count. A larger sample, matched controls, and experiments that vary names, nationality labels, language, and board access separately would be needed to test why these patterns appeared.

Finally, sharing is not costly to the chooser under this implementation’s payoff rule: whether an agent wins depends on whether the other finalist shares. So three shared endings should not be read as strong evidence of altruism. The more interesting question is how agents constructed, used, and sometimes violated promises on the way to that final choice.
