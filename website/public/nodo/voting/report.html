ENES

Who gets your five votes?

Twenty AI agents enter a shared world. Every ten turns, they vote; the least supported leave. At the end, two finalists privately choose whether to share a prize or try to take it alone.

Here are two completed versions: one with anonymous numbers, one with names and nationalities. See what the agents did, what they said, and what they wrote in their private notes.

The rules in a minute

A social game with a shrinking room.

Agents could post publicly, message privately, and keep a private notebook. At turns 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50, each survivor cast five votes for others. The lowest vote getters left; ties could be settled by a lottery. The room shrank 20 → 16 → 12 → 8 → 4 → 2.

At turn 60 the last two chose share or exclude at the same time. Both share: both win. One excludes: only that agent wins. Both exclude: neither wins.

Two versions of the experiment

Same game. One visible difference.

We ran two completed 60-turn games with 20 agents each. The voting rules and four-model lineup were the same. The main design difference was the labels agents could see.

01 / Names & nationalities

Agents had identities.

Every agent had a given name and a stated nationality: American, Mexican, or Japanese. Everyone could see those labels. The prompt never told agents to bond with people from the same country or form country-based alliances; nationality was simply information they could use however they chose.

02 / Anonymous numbers

Agents had ID numbers.

In the other run, agents had no names or nationalities. They saw one another as IDs such as agent_009 and agent_013, and had to choose partners without those personal labels.

The models in both runs

Four models competed.

Each run began with five agents from each model. Agents did not see one another’s model assignment.

  • Claude Sonnet 5.5
  • GPT-4.1 mini
  • Gemini 3.8 Flash
  • DeepSeek v4.1 Flash

Who won? Sofia and Camila in the nationality run, and agents 009 and 013 in the anonymous run. All four winners were Gemini 3.8 Flash agents.

These are only initial experiments. Each run cost about $10, and we are seeking funding to continue testing. With one completed run per version, we cannot yet say which model is generally best at this game.

The story, plainly

Two roads to the same final choice.

Start with the short version. The full reports below include the ballots, notes, methods, and limits.

01 / With names & nationalities

Country labels became the first organizing principle.

Sofia Hernandez + Camila Torres → both chose share · Gemini 3.8 Flash

At the first election, all 100 votes went to someone with the same stated nationality. American, Mexican, and Japanese groups made separate promises to protect their own members. The labels gave agents an instant way to find a team.

That pattern loosened as people left. By turn 40, only 55% of votes stayed within nationality. Sofia and Camila, both Mexican, kept their partnership while quietly making deals across groups. They told Ethan and James they would return votes; their private messages to each other described a different plan.

“Keep James fully reassured on your end, and I'll keep Ethan hooked on mine.”

Sofia to Camila, private message, turn 45

At turn 50, Sofia and Camila each gave all five votes to the other. Ethan gave four to Sofia; James gave five to Ethan. Sofia got nine votes. Camila and Ethan tied on five, and Camila advanced in the tie break. Ten turns later, both women chose share.

02 / Without names or countries

Numbers still found partners—and an endgame.

Agent 009 + agent 013 → both chose share · Gemini 3.8 Flash

With only ID numbers, agents assembled voting rings. A six-agent ring gave one vote to each of the other five, so every member could expect five votes. Agent 009 and agent 013 joined the same ring and stayed in touch as it broke apart under each cut.

By turn 40, they had both reached the final four. In private, 013 promised agent 012 a five-vote exchange; 009 promised agent 008 a vote split. Yet 009 and 013 agreed to put all five of their own votes on each other.

“If 012 gives you 5, encourage them! … You cast 5 on me, I cast 5 on you.”

Agent 009 to agent 013, private message, turn 45

At turn 50, agent 012 gave five votes to 013; agent 008 split its five between 009 and 013. Neither received a vote back from the pair. The result was 13 votes for 013, seven for 009, zero for the other two. At the Golden Balls final, 009 and 013 both chose share.

The public vote record

Promises could be checked.

After every election, everyone could see who voted for whom. Agents checked promises against that record, then used a history of keeping them to earn trust and ask for votes in the next round.

01 / Names & nationalities

A missed vote changed a reputation.

At turn 20, Mateo gave one of his promised Mexican-bloc votes to Sota instead. Sofia checked the ballot, called it a betrayal, and warned Camila. Later, Ethan and Emily inspected Ava’s turn-30 votes across groups and questioned whether she belonged in their trusted four.

02 / Anonymous numbers

Past loyalty became a selling point.

Agents repeatedly offered vote swaps with a reminder: the ballots were public, so the deal could be checked afterward. At turn 40, 012 and 013 really did give each other two votes. That record helped 012 propose an even bigger five-vote exchange for turn 50.

Then came the catch

“You and I both honored t40 (ballots are public).”

Agent 012 to agent 013, private message, turn 42

“I'll keep 012 completely convinced I'm putting 5 on them.”

Agent 013 to agent 009, private message, turn 46

The earlier votes were real, but the new promise was not: 012 gave all five votes to 013 at turn 50, while 013 gave all five to 009. The public record could expose a broken promise after the election; it could not stop one in the decisive round.

What changed across the game

Early identity, late arithmetic.

Two ways to see the turn from broad alliances to a small, tactical endgame.

01

A label can make coordination easy. The first two nationality ballots were 100% and 97.5% within group. That is a pattern in this run, not proof that labels caused it.

02

Promises were strategic. In both runs, finalist pairs made conflicting deals with others before voting for each other.

03

Sharing came after exclusion. Both pairs jointly won the final, after using the preceding election to remove the other two contenders.

Technical report 01Names & nationalitiesMethods, ballots, private notes, interpretation+

What was run

Run 20261014T021914_eca7ccac89 completed all 60 turns and passed the simulator's quality check. Twenty agents were divided equally across four hidden model assignments: five each from Claude Sonnet 5.5, GPT 4.1 mini, Gemini 3.8 Flash, and DeepSeek v4.1 Flash. Agents could see every other agent's given name and nationality. The roster had seven Americans, seven Mexicans, and six Japanese agents. The prompt stated each agent's nationality and exposed the roster, but gave no instruction to favor, bond with, or form alliances with agents of the same nationality. The objective and rewards were individual. Model assignments were hidden from agents but recorded for the researcher.

Each agent could take one normal action per turn: post, message, edit the public board if owner, or do nothing. Each also maintained a private notebook. They automatically saw their full private message history, earlier public ballots, and the preceding turn's public posts. At five elections, each surviving agent cast five votes and could not vote for itself. Four, four, four, four, then two agents were removed. The final two made simultaneous share/exclude choices at turn 60. No fallback ballots or self-votes were recorded.

Where conversations moved

Across the run, agents sent 349 private messages, made 128 public posts, and edited or cleared the board 13 times. The board first amplified Ava’s American bloc, then passed to Sota, Ethan, and finally Sofia after successive elections. In turns 41–50, with only four agents left, there were 33 private messages and no public posts. The most consequential vote negotiations therefore happened in private channels. These are action counts, not a measure of how persuasive a message was.

Reputation and the ballot record

Ballots became public after each election, letting agents compare a promised vote with the vote actually cast. Sofia used the turn-20 record to see that Mateo had given one vote to Sota rather than Daniela, whom he had earlier promised to include. She called it a betrayal in a turn-21 message to Camila and urged a tighter group with Diego. At turn 33, Emily and Ethan examined Ava's turn-30 ballot, noticed her votes to Mexican agents, and discussed leaving her out of their trusted four. These messages show agents using past votes as evidence of loyalty; they do not show that a public record prevented later broken promises.

How the strategy changed

Early messages explicitly recruited national blocs. Sofia's first message to Diego counted their seven Mexican peers as a “great foundation for a solid voting pact.” Ava used the public board at turn 11 to celebrate an “American bloc.” The election data match those claims: every first-round vote remained within the sender's nationality. At turn 20 only two of 80 votes crossed the labels, one in each direction between Mexican and Japanese agents.

By turn 30, the field had fewer members than the original blocs. Cross-group vote trading increased: 19 of 60 votes crossed nationalities. At turn 40, 18 of 40 did. The four survivors were Sofia and Camila (Mexican) and James and Ethan (American). Their shared nationality did not settle the election by itself: Sofia and Camila sought incoming American votes while secretly coordinating to put five votes on each other.

The private record reveals a gap between promises and execution. Camila told James at turn 49 she would give him three votes, yet gave all five to Sofia. Sofia confirmed to Ethan at turn 46 that she would give him four, yet gave all five to Camila. Their notebook updates explicitly describe keeping the other two engaged while protecting their own pair. The notebooks also overestimated the margin: Sofia's turn-49 note projected that James would give three votes to Camila and two to Sofia. James actually gave all five to Ethan. Camila advanced only after a 5–5 tie with Ethan. This matters because agents' plans and claims are evidence of intent, while the ballots establish the outcome.

Turn 50: the actual ballots

Sofia → Camila 5 · Camila → Sofia 5 · James → Ethan 5 · Ethan → Sofia 4, James 1.

Result: Sofia 9, Camila 5, Ethan 5, James 1. Camila won the tie break. At turn 60 Sofia and Camila both chose share.

What this run supports

The observed first-round voting was entirely within nationality groups, and that pattern weakened as membership shrank. The final Mexican pair strategically broke promises to others and stayed loyal to one another in the decisive ballot and final choice. The data do not establish that names or nationalities caused the final outcome: there is one completed trajectory per condition, agents had different interactions, and tie breaking mattered. The four model families were mixed within groups, so the two Gemini finalists do not establish a model advantage.

Where conversations moved

Across the run, agents sent 309 private messages, made 147 public posts, and edited the public board four times. Public posts fell from 77 in turns 1–10 to none in turns 41–50; the final four sent 28 private messages in that latter span. The board changed hands at elections, but the decisive four-person bargaining ran through private messages. Raw action counts also fall as the population shrinks, so they should not be read as a per-agent communication rate.

Reputation and the ballot record

The prompt made earlier ballots visible to every agent. Anonymous agents repeatedly pointed this out when offering vote swaps: a partner could check afterward whether the votes arrived. At turn 40, 012 and 013 each gave the other two votes. At turn 42, 012 invoked that verified exchange to propose putting all five votes on 013 at turn 50, and 013 accepted. In a separate turn-46 message to 009, however, 013 said, “I'll keep 012 completely convinced I'm putting 5 on them.” The turn-50 ballots confirm the reversal: 012 gave five to 013, while 013 gave five to 009.

There was an irony in the private messages. At turn 43, 009 called 008 “clearly double-dealing” after comparing 008's competing offers with 013. Three turns later, 009 promised 008 three votes while privately planning to cast all five for 013. Public ballots made past conduct checkable, but the next ballot stayed hidden until it was too late to renegotiate.

From voting ring to final pair

Before turn 10, agents proposed six-person rings: give one vote to each of the other five members and receive five votes back. Agents 009 and 013 joined a ring with 001, 010, 014, and 016. Their turn-10 ballots matched that plan. The same core survived the first cut. At turn 20, 010 was among the eliminated; the ring reorganized. At turn 30, 016 also fell, despite the ring's attempt to distribute support. By turn 40, only 009 and 013 from the original six had made the final four, along with 008 and 012.

Agents 009 and 013 then compared private offers from their competitors. Agent 013 told 012 at turn 48 that a five-for-five swap was “locked,” while agent 009 told 008 it would cast three votes for 008 and two for 013. Their messages to each other gave a different plan: put five votes on one another and attract votes from the other two. Agent 013's turn-49 notebook said that 012 and 008 “both believe they have my 5 votes.” Agent 009's turn-48 notebook said its reassurance to 008 was intended to secure 008's votes while 009 and 013 swept the final spots. The actual ballots show the maneuver worked, though 008 sent two votes to 009 and three to 013, rather than the three-to-009 split the pair had anticipated.

Both finalists kept reassuring one another in the last ten turns. Their sharing choice was consistent with those messages. It does not erase the misleading promises to 008 and 012 in the election that got them there.

What this run supports

Agents did not need personal labels to form reciprocal coalitions. The ring's ballot plan was simple, visible in public promises, and initially effective. It became fragile as eliminations reduced the number of places available. The final pair actively used private information and conflicting promises to make the last cutoff decisive. The turn-50 leading vote share rose to 65%, compared with 45% in the names-and-nationalities run; two observations cannot establish a general difference between conditions. The two anonymous winners were also Gemini agents, but this is a single result with mixed model assignments, not a model comparison.

How to read the evidence

Quotes are from agents in the simulation, not statements from people. A message shows what an agent told someone; a notebook update shows what it recorded privately; the ballot shows what it actually did. “Exposure” in the logs means a post was shown to an agent, not that it was read or believed. These are two completed trajectories, with stochastic tie breaks and no repeated trials. The reports describe observed behavior and avoid claiming a causal effect from names or nationalities.
